# Dataset and calculation policy

This is a manually curated launch calendar for **OpenAI, Anthropic, Google (Gemini), DeepSeek and Meta**, observed from 2023-01-01 through **2026-10-19**, inclusive. The expanded edition and the October 3–19 update window were researched on 2026-10-19. It is not a live feed, an exhaustive industry inventory, or a benchmark-based definition of the frontier. Other labs are outside this edition's scope; lab order is OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, DeepSeek, Meta.

## Unit and inclusion

One event is a selected, publicly announced launch or rollout from one lab on one calendar date. Same-day model variants from that lab are grouped, rather than counted as separate points. Events from different labs on the same date remain separate. Dates use the publisher's stated calendar date and are represented internally at UTC midnight; exact launch times and regional rollouts are not inferred.

The sample follows general-purpose GPT / o-series and Claude main model releases and explicitly announced major revisions. Public previews are included and marked. Same-name ChatGPT model updates and later availability are excluded, as with the GPT-4o updates of 2024–2025. It includes Sonnet, Opus and publicly available Fable releases, alongside the relevant general-purpose OpenAI families. For Google, it follows the Gemini line: each new generation or version number (1.0, 1.5, 2.0, 2.5, 3, 3.1, 3.5–3.8, 4 Argon) and the first public release of the Ultra tier. A Flash model counts only when it opens a new version number; Gemini 3.6, 3.7 and 3.8 Flash were Google's main general-purpose releases after 3.1 Pro and are included. It does not assert that every included model was the top-scoring frontier model at its launch.

Excluded categories: standalone mini/nano/Haiku/Instant-small variants, coding-specific Codex variants, open-weight GPT-oss, image/audio/video models, product and tool launches, pricing or prompt-only updates, compute-only pro modes, routine same-name API snapshots, and later availability on additional platforms. “GPT-5.3 Instant” is a newly named general-purpose release and is included; the older lightweight Claude Instant line is excluded. Closed partner evaluations and restricted Mythos releases are excluded. Announced but unreleased models are excluded. For Google this excludes Flash-Lite, Live and TTS audio models, image and video models (Nano Banana, Omni), open-weight Gemma, Deep Think modes, dated experimental snapshots, restricted Cyber / Fairwind-only access (the 2026-09-30 Argon phase is excluded, while its 2026-10-13 public release is included), the announced but unreleased Gemini 3.5 Pro, and pre-Gemini Bard / PaLM models.

This is an editorial sample, not a rule that guarantees completeness. Its value is a traceable, editable visualization. A comparison of the entire market would require a broader, separately reviewed selection.

## Main families, access and the expanded labs

The common unit is a main general-purpose language/reasoning model family and a materially new public model revision. Open weights are neither required nor sufficient. Llama and DeepSeek LLM/V/R are the respective labs' main families and are included; GPT-oss and Gemma remain auxiliary open-weight lines of primarily closed-model labs and remain excluded. Meta's main MSL successor families (Muse Spark and Wonder) are included even when weights are closed. Muse Spark Open releases the existing Spark model as weights with adapted safety post-training, so it is excluded as a new availability channel. With Muse now Meta's closed main line, open weights alone do not establish another main revision; this also keeps the auxiliary/open-weight boundary consistent with GPT-oss and Gemma. Muse Glimmer is a small on-device adjunct and is excluded by the same small/auxiliary-line rule as other labs. “Open-weight” describes availability, not an OSI license certification: initial LLaMA was research-only, Llama 2–4 use custom Community Licenses, early DeepSeek weights use a custom Model License, R1 and later V revisions use MIT, and Spark Open uses the custom Muse Model License. ### What this comparison adds

Retrospective ECI matching shows Llama 2 70B at 113.84 versus GPT-4 at 125.88 in July 2023 (12.04-point difference), then Llama 3.1 405B at 128.76 versus GPT-4o May at 129.05 in July 2024 (0.29). Thus the sampled open-weight main tier had largely closed that particular gap by July 2024. This is a comparison of selected released checkpoints, not all open/closed models, a causal claim or a statistically significant ranking. DeepSeek R1 (138.96) versus o1 (141.84) in January 2025 similarly narrows to 2.88 points; both gaps are within or close to ECI's published uncertainty. Meta's 2026 Spark/Wonder transition also shows why company identity must not be treated as an immutable open-weight/closed-weight classification.

The camera follows the calendar and recent score distribution with smooth, gradual framing, then reveals the whole chart once at the observation cutoff. All per-kick zoom, four-bar sinusoidal motion, groove-start pullbacks, launch punch-ins and shake are removed; there is no drop shake. Its state remains a pure function of time. Decorative animation never displaces the data point or quantizes release dates.

## Capability scores

Burst heights use the [Epoch Capabilities Index (ECI)](https://epoch.ai/benchmarks/eci) published by Epoch AI under [CC BY 4.0](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/4.0/). Scores were copied from Epoch's [CSV export](https://epoch.ai/data/eci_scores.csv) on 2026-10-19 and are stored with each record as `capability: { model, score }`. ECI combines many benchmarks onto one scale, so models from 2023 and 2026 can be compared directly. Epoch publishes an uncertainty interval several points wide for each model; a gap of a few points is not a ranking.

Mapping rules:

- The official publisher date controls the plotted launch day. Epoch CSV dates differ for the same named checkpoints: V2 May 6 vs May 7, Llama 3.2 Sept 25 vs Sept 24, Llama 4 April 5 vs April 6, V4.1 Flash Sept 10 vs Sept 9, and Argon Oct 13 public release vs Sept 30 restricted introduction. Notes preserve these differences; score matching still uses the exact version, rather than altering launch dates to match a benchmark table.

- Use the ECI entry for the model version released in that event. Dated variants must match: GPT-4 → `GPT-4 (Mar 2023)`, GPT-4 Turbo → `GPT-4 Turbo (Nov 2023)`, GPT-4o → `GPT-4o (May 2024)`, Claude 3.5 Sonnet v2 → `Claude 3.5 Sonnet (October 2024)`, Gemini 2.0 Flash → `Gemini 2.0 Flash (Dec 2024)` (the experimental release), Gemini 2.5 Pro → `Gemini 2.5 Pro (Mar 2025)`.
- When an event groups several tiers, use its top tier: Claude 3 → Claude 3 Opus, Claude 4 → Claude Opus 4, GPT-5.6 → GPT-5.6 Sol. Gemini 1.0 uses Gemini 1.0 Pro, the tier that shipped that day. Separately sold compute tiers (GPT-5.x Pro, Deep Think) are not used, matching the event selection.
- o1 uses Epoch's `o1` entry, which was evaluated on the `o1-2024-12-17` API snapshot of the model launched on December 5.
- Never substitute a later snapshot. Gemini 1.5 Pro (February 2024 preview) has no score because Epoch only lists the May and September 2024 versions.

Unscored as of 2026-10-19 (8 events): Claude (2023), Gemini Ultra 1.0, Gemini 1.5 Pro (February preview), DeepSeek V2.5, R1-Lite-Preview, GPT-5.3 Instant, Muse Wonder Full and DeepSeek V4.1 Flash-1017. Only GPT-5.3 Instant retains a pending name mapping from the earlier edition; new events without an exact CSV entry have no `capability` field. Do not invent a score or substitute a different checkpoint. V3.2 now uses the general `DeepSeek-V3.2` row (146.11); its grouped specialist Speciale does not control score selection. GPT-6 Sol (September) and GPT-6.1 Sol have scores, 162.55 and 166.32.

`npm run scores -- --retrieved 2026-10-19` re-downloaded Epoch's public CSV and refreshed every mapped record, including all 51 original records. 71 of 79 events are scored; 42 original records changed (including two newly scored). The full before/after table is in the [2026-10-19 review](reviews/2026-10-19-update.md). `retrievedOn` is the retrieval date of this retrospective index, not the date on which a score was knowable at launch.

## Metrics

For each year, intersect the calendar year with the dataset's observation window:

```text
observed_days = inclusive_end_day - inclusive_start_day + 1
days_per_launch = observed_days / selected_launch_count
mean_adjacent_gap = (last_event_day - first_event_day) / (event_count - 1)
```

The conclusion presents **days per launch**, a density measure. It is not the mean adjacent gap, the duration of training, or a prediction of the next release. Leap years use 366 days. A zero-event year has no density estimate; zero- and one-event years have no mean-gap estimate. Same-day events contribute a zero-day adjacent gap.

| Observation | Days | Events | Days / event |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 2023 | 365 | 9 | 40.6 |
| 2024 | 366 | 17 | 21.5 |
| 2025 | 365 | 21 | 17.4 |
| 2026-01-01–2026-10-19 | 292 | 32 | 9.1 |

The 2026 result is year-to-date, so it is not directly interchangeable with a completed year's cadence. The original post's “18 days” is not treated as verified evidence and is not hard-coded. The rendering and generated statistics always recompute values from the JSON.

### Launch pace (the indicator panel)

During the calendar travel, a panel beside the chart (under it in portrait) plots a smoothed launch rate, read like an indicator with a price chart:

```text
pace(t) = 365 / τ · Σ exp(−(t − d_i) / τ)   over selected launch dates d_i ≤ t,   τ = 60 days
```

The unit is launches per year, annualized: the rate the recent launches would add up to over a year. Each launch adds about 6.1 on its date, and its contribution falls to 37% after 60 days; a steady cadence of one launch every g days settles to an average of 365 / g. Dotted gridlines mark those averages for one launch every 60, 30, 14 and 7 days (6, 12, 26 and 52 a year), labelled with both values. The pace only looks back. Same-day launches from different labs count separately, as in the density table, and each launch also leaves its lab-shaped mark on the panel's baseline. Launches before the observation window are not in the dataset, so the curve starts at zero and understates the pace in early 2023. It is a display smoothing of this curated sample, not a statistical estimate of industry activity or a forecast. In this dataset it ends at about 62.8 a year (one launch every 5.8 days), above the 2026 year-to-date average of 40.0 a year (9.1 days per launch) because late September and October 2026 were unusually dense; a shorter time constant would end higher and a longer one lower. The panel's vertical scale is 1.35 times the highest pace reached so far (at least 18), so it widens whenever the pace sets a new high and the gridlines sink as launches speed up.

## Visual time

The main stage is a two-dimensional chart. The horizontal axis is one continuous calendar from January 1 of the first year to December 31 of the last; dates after the observation cutoff are hatched. The vertical axis is the ECI score, with gridlines every 10 points and headroom above the highest score. Each rocket climbs straight up from its exact date and bursts at its score, then leaves a marker. A step line per lab follows that lab's highest score so far, rising when a launch sets a new high. Launches without a score spray a low fountain on the date axis instead of taking a height. Each burst has the same 68 rays, radius and 1.9-second lifetime. Five stable lab-specific burst forms and five marker shapes distinguish the labs in the legend, plot and ledger without relying on color. The original three colors remain unchanged; DeepSeek uses copper and Meta rose. Palette checks apply the Machado et al. (2009) full-severity protanopia and deuteranopia matrices in linear sRGB, then compare Oklab distances; the reproducible contact sheet and pairwise distances are in `output/qa/`. These simulations support design review, not a universal perception guarantee. Burst positions are never shifted for legibility; late 2026 launches overlap because they are close in date and score. In crowded moments, only decorative bloom/trails dim by a shared inverse-square-root crowd factor; point geometry, ray count, size and lifetime stay unchanged. Callouts last up to 1.6 seconds and are drawn above bursts and step lines on a dark backing plate with a thin lab-colored border. They group launches within 0.3 seconds into at most three rows, with an overflow count that appears only once the extra launches occur. The launch-pace panel has the chart's width and shares its calendar and camera: it pans and zooms horizontally with the chart, while its vertical scale follows the pace alone. In landscape it sits beside the chart at the same height, so both get the full plot height; in portrait it sits under the date axis. Its baseline uses each lab's marker shape for every launch, including unscored ones; five labs use five tightly spaced lanes. Previews retain an asterisk and hollow marker. The large year counter sits in the plot's upper left, which early (low) scores leave empty.

The playback clock has 45 seconds of continuous, linear calendar travel, a 3.75-second introduction, a 3.75-second final hold, and a 7.5-second conclusion: 60 seconds, exactly 32 bars of the 128 BPM score. A calendar day always has the same film duration, including across leap years. The clock stops at the observation cutoff. There are no release-date pauses or late-film speedups. Active bursts retain their full lifetime across year transitions, using their own event year for geometry. Markers and step lines persist and remain faintly behind the conclusion; they are not additional launches. The conclusion also shows the highest ECI among selected launches in the first and last year. The burst and its sound occur at the date's onset; the upward trail and sound start 0.36 seconds earlier. Same-day labs burst simultaneously. Intro/outro are presentation sections outside the calendar clock.

Both layouts allocate four rows per lab, with a multi-row card grid where needed. Each lab's list is ordered newest first, with the ECI score rounded to an integer at the right (“—” when unscored), and retains the latest events across year transitions. A new arrival enters at the top and pushes the older names down one row over 0.28 seconds; the fifth name fades out. Close arrivals stack without overlapping mid-animation. Each record stays among its lab's four newest for at least 1.4 seconds; a test checks this against the dataset. The newest names stay on screen through the final hold. Dates remain on the cards. Compact cards (portrait, or landscape with four/five labs) omit the repetitive Claude prefix when a family name follows, the DeepSeek prefix before V/R numbered names, and the year in month qualifiers; purely numeric Claude names such as Claude 2.1 remain intact. All real model labels fit without ellipsis at the tested minimum size; synthetic unusually long names may use an ellipsis. The source ledger, callouts and captions preserve full names. Future denser datasets that break the 1.4-second minimum will fail the test, requiring a longer calendar travel duration or more rows.

Audio and video share the same logical clock. The renderer maps the last video frame to the logical ending; audio cue times use exactly the same `(frames - 1) / fps / logical_duration` scale. Intro/outro frame rounding does not accumulate drift. The 128 BPM score's sections (intro, travel, arrival, conclusion) start on bar lines; launch cues are not quantized to the beat. See [audio synthesis and mastering](audio.md).

## Updating and verification

Open each proposed primary source and check the model name, stated date, launch/preview status, and access distinction. Keep a note when sources disagree.
